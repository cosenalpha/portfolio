Ghosts in the machine
Apr 2025 - Jul 2026
This project, my master's thesis, investigates how synthetic data can instead become a political tool resisting epistemic violence in design pedagogy, particularly for future scenario creation.
In times of crisis, imagining futures becomes crucial for understanding how we want our world to develop. Design bears responsibility to support futuring practices, yet as methods evolve, examining how emerging tools can enable more decolonial approaches becomes essential.

Artificial intelligence presents opportunities for design education, increasingly used for speculation and research. However, current AI systems, built on datasets historically extracted and organized through colonialist frameworks, perpetuate epistemic violence by marginalizing non-Western perspectives. This is particularly concerning in design education, where Western programs already reproduce capitalist-colonial worldviews. Design futuring itself emerged from Western-centric traditions demonstrating limitations in integrating pluriversal perspectives, making biased AI integration especially problematic.

As design recognizes the necessity of centering marginalized communities, uncritical AI integration risks entrenching Western approaches while excluding pluralistic ways of knowing, reinforcing hierarchies where dominant systems prevail and alternative futures remain unimagined.

Synthetic data has emerged as a proposed solution through debiasing processes. However, this approach doesn't resist colonialism but complies with it, merely changing how industry satisfies data needs while perpetuating value-neutral framings that obscure data's political nature. This project investigates how synthetic data can instead become a political tool resisting epistemic violence in design pedagogy, particularly for future scenario creation. By inverting debiasing logic to amplify rather than eliminate biases, this research proposes critical reflection on both user assumptions and AI system limitations. Through research-through-design, this work presents the design and anticipated outcomes of a pedagogical framework using an interface to support students in creating future scenarios while revealing how collective inputs encode colonial patterns.

The framework will be evaluated through a one-day workshop (May 2026) demonstrating how participatory synthetic dataset creation makes epistemic violence visible through deliberate tool failure, fostering decolonial literacy and reflexive practices among design students, transforming synthetic data from technical fix into critical pedagogy tool.
